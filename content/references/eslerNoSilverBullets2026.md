I am working on a small writing project regarding fads in mission for a book that will publish sometime in the next year. It has me thinking about the many conversations I have had with people about mission strategy.

Just last month, I met a donor who is quite generously supporting indigenous missionary work. May his tribe increase - we need many more donors like him. In the course of the conversation, he emphasized ROI. “If mission investment was done with a greater emphasis on ROI,” he said, “we’d be further along.”

Maybe.

ROI is a terrible evaluator of mission effectiveness. Whenever this topic comes up, I ask donors if they would have cut checks to support Isaiah for his highly ineffective ministry. Or if they would have emptied out an entire vial of expensive perfume on Jesus’ feet.

Trust me, I want to fund effective strategies. But what we think of as effective, versus what is actually effective, and how God executes on mission are often very different things.

As the global church continues to wallow at around the same percentage of global population as it has been at for the past century, it is worth reflecting on the hardness of the task before us. There are many varied and distinct approaches at work.

None of them are silver bullets.

Here’s a list of concepts that people have given over the years that they think are going to “solve” the problem of the Great Commission.

- Diaspora
- Funding Nationals
- Business as Mission
- Traditional Church Planting
- Movements
- Mass Evangelism
- Training
- Education
- Bible Translation
- Digital Ministry
- Humanitarian Aid, Relief and Development
- Culturally Near Missions

I could go on… just about any type of solution is wanting. Every single one of these could be an effective and important element, but none of them are strategies that will “solve” the Great Commission.

I find myself falling prey to thinking that missions is a problem to be solved. I tend to believe in focusing on unreached people group ministry. Is it strategic? Yes, it is. Is it more strategic than what others might be doing? No - it is one of many ways that God is working in the world. We need to give a lot of grace for the Holy Spirit to work out the Great Commission in the hearts of people the way He sees fit.

### Why do we search for silver bullets?

Why do we so often insist on promoting a particular strategy as the silver bullet? There are a couple of drivers. One is certainly the quest to get people to pay attention. We are living in a world where there are literally thousands of messages sent your way each and every day. The more outlandish and outrageous our claims are, the more likely we are going to be able to pull attention onto our own pet strategy.

Another big reason we promote our own missions strategy is pride and its close cousin, envy. Pride appears in a lot of different, evil ways within missions. If you’ve ever been on a church planting team with other language learners, you will know what pride on the mission field feels like. When somebody arrives and, six months later, is fluently yapping in the local language (while you’ve spent two years and are still struggling) your pride will make an appearance. It’s very hard not to compare yourself to others.

Theological pride is yet another reason we can lose sight of the many ways that God is working in the world. This used to be mostly manifested along denominational lines. I’m grateful to say that I don’t see this at play as much as it apparently was in past decades. Today, it seems to fall more along the lines of church planting strategies, particularly with the traditional church planting crowd. They believe they have figured out how Jesus wants it done. If you’re not doing it their way, you are doing it the wrong way, and they don’t want you showing up at their conferences and events. But don’t be too harsh on them. They’re in good company when it comes to thinking that they have it right.

Organizational pride is another way we lose sight of the larger goal. When we start believing our own brochures, we can get into trouble. The success of others should be our success. I am ashamed to admit it, but I feel this kind of pride and envy all too often. When I hear about the success of other mission networks, it is very easy for me to feel that jab of competition. I should be cheering them all on. We need so many more collaborations in mission.

There is some naivety at work as well. I’m actually a huge believer in naivety. Had I known what I would eventually go through as a cross-cultural worker, I’m not sure I would have been so eager as I had been to sign up. It was a good thing I was naive. Yet naivety also plays a role in overestimating our favored mission strategies.

Here is a rather extreme example. Soon after I first arrived in Ontario to lead Pioneers Canada we received a fax about a new strategy called “Fax the Gospel.” The fax stated that they were collecting numbers from every country in the world and that, within a few months, they would reach all nations on the planet by faxing the gospel into them. The second page of the fax was a simple gospel presentation. The claim was that they would fulfill the prophecies of Scripture and usher in the reign of Jesus by completing the Great Commission in short order through faxing. At first, I didn’t think it could be real, but after following up, I realized that it was, in fact, a real, one-man organization and he really believed that this strategy would complete the task. Bless him, but that is a pretty naive strategy.

#### If there are no silver bullets, what is there?

I have this book on my desk with the title “700 Plans to Evangelize the World” by Barret and Reapsome. It was published in 1988. By now, that book would probably be called “7,000 Plans to Evangelize the World.” It is pretty hard to dream up new ideas that have never been attempted before in global mission. That doesn’t mean we don’t try. In fact, I would say quite the opposite. We need vastly more innovation and creativity.

In the final analysis missions is spiritual work. If it’s really the Holy Spirit that changes people’s hearts, then let’s have a humble posture about our strategies. We need to cultivate healthy dependence and expectation that God is at work, while making the best decisions we can. We have to trust that others are pursuing the same.

Because in the end, there are no silver bullets in mission.
