## Definition

"Brain science" as a concept thread in this vault refers to the body of neuroscience research that has overturned Enlightenment assumptions about how humans form identity, make decisions, and change behavior. The key finding: attachment and relational experience shape the brain's wiring more than rational deliberation does.

Several converging research lines build this picture. Norman Doidge's [[doidgeBrainThatChanges2007|The Brain That Changes Itself]] established neuroplasticity as settled science—the brain physically rewires in response to experience throughout life, not just in childhood. Antonio Damasio's *Descartes' Error* (1994) showed that emotions aren't noise in the decision-making process; they're integral to it. Patients with damage to emotional processing centers made catastrophically bad decisions despite intact reasoning ability.

Allan Schore's attachment research took this further: the right hemisphere develops through relational attunement with caregivers, and this relational wiring shapes identity at a level deeper than conscious thought. [[warnerSolutionChoice2018|The Solution of Choice]] summarizes the sequence as: Attachment → Assessment → Attunement → Action/Identity. All of this processing happens before the left brain's conscious evaluation begins.

[[kahnemanThinkingFastSlow2013|Thinking, Fast and Slow]] provides the cognitive framework: [[MOC Fast track and slow track|System 1]] (fast, automatic, pattern-based) handles most of life, while System 2 (slow, deliberate, effortful) plays a supporting role. Most of what we call "character" operates at the System 1 level—in habits, automatic responses, and identity patterns formed through relationships.

## Why This Matters

The practical upshot is that transformation through information transfer alone doesn't work. You can't lecture someone into a new identity. Behavior change that lasts happens through relational attachment, repeated experience, and the slow rewiring of automatic responses. This has implications for education, discipleship, therapy, organizational change, and communication strategy.
