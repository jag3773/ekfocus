# Servant Selling Meets the Dependency Critique of Funded Mission

Two convictions in the vault sit in productive tension. Daniel Pink's [[Servant Selling]] holds that persuasion is virtuous when it serves the other person, and offers a clean test: if the person agrees, will their life improve, and will the world be better when the exchange ends? On that test, raising money for a worthy mission looks like an unambiguous good. The benefactor gives, the cause advances, both parties and the world come out ahead.

The [[2026-05 Kingdom Funding Literature Review]] complicates that confidence. Its sources argue that external funding tends toward dependency by default rather than by accident: funded leaders begin optimizing for the donor's expectations, professional structures crowd out reproducible simplicity, and Western money carries Western control even when no one intends it. On this reading, a perfectly executed, genuinely well-meant fundraising "sale" can still leave the recipient community worse off, because the harm lives in the structure of the relationship rather than in the seller's heart. The servant-selling test, which judges intent and immediate benefit, can return a passing grade on an exchange whose downstream effect is corrosive.

The tension does not cancel either view; it sharpens both. Pink's framework supplies the relational ethics of the ask, while the funding literature supplies the structural ethics of what the money then does. A fuller test for moving a benefactor might add a third question to Pink's two: not only "will the giver's life improve" and "will the world be better," but "will this gift increase or decrease the recipient's long-term agency?" That keeps servant selling honest in exactly the place the dependency critique says good intentions most often fail.
