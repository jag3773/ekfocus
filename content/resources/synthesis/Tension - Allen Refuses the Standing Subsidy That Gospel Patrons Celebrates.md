# Tension - Allen Refuses the Standing Subsidy That Gospel Patrons Celebrates

The vault already holds a patronage dispute drawn from Paul, but Roland Allen sharpens it from a second direction and against a different target. In [[allenMissionaryMethodsSt2011|Missionary Methods]] Allen makes the missionary's financial restraint a method, not a preference. His reading of [[Paul's Three Money Rules Left Every Church to Fund and Govern Itself|Paul's three money rules]] yields a categorical refusal: the worker carries no funds to the people he evangelizes and never administers their money, so each church funds and governs itself from birth. Allen then names the cost of breaking the rule. When the mission supplies what the local church cannot, [[Imported Buildings and Subsidy Pauperize Converts Into Passive Recipients|imported buildings and subsidy pauperize the converts into passive recipients]] and build up the very foreign dependence the mission should most want to destroy.

[[John Rinehart]]'s [[Gospel Patrons - Summary|Gospel Patrons]] celebrates the figure Allen warns against. Rinehart's heroes do not seed a work and step back; they sustain ongoing operations from the outside. Lady Huntingdon underwrote 116 preaching places and a seminary, and [[A Patron Funded and Smuggled the First English Bible|Humphrey Monmouth funded, sheltered, and smuggled Tyndale's New Testament]] across England. Rinehart reads these standing subsidies as the forgotten back half of a gospel team and treats the patron-worker bond as a model to recover. Where Allen sees imported funding pauperizing a young church into passivity, Rinehart sees a benefactor making the otherwise impossible work happen.

The disagreement is genuine and not dissolved by good intentions. It differs from the existing [[Tension - Rinehart Celebrates the Patronage Paul Deliberately Refused|Rinehart-versus-Paul tension]] by pitting a field missiologist's positive method against the patron model rather than reconstructing Paul's motive: Allen is not merely noting that Paul declined money, he is prescribing that the missionary must decline to import or administer it because subsidy itself deforms the recipient church. The fault line runs through scale and duration. Both prize generosity, and Rinehart himself blunts the edge when he reports that Lady Huntingdon often encouraged local people to fund their own buildings and then added a launching gift, a seed-not-subsidy posture Allen's three-self logic would actually endorse. The unresolved core is whether sustained external funding of the work, as opposed to a one-time seed, can ever leave a church self-supporting, or whether, as Allen insists, it trains dependence by its very nature.
