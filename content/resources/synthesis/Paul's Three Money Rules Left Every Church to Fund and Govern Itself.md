# Paul's Three Money Rules Left Every Church to Fund and Govern Itself

Allen distills Paul's financial conduct into three rules in [[allenMissionaryMethodsSt2011|Missionary Methods]]: Paul sought no financial help for himself from those he evangelized, carried no financial support to them, and never administered local church funds. The point was never asceticism. Paul welcomed gifts from the Philippians and took wages from other churches, but he refused money from the people he was actively preaching to, so that no one could suspect he came to make money. Every province and every church stood financially independent from the start. No congregation depended on another, with the single exception of the relief collection for the poor saints at Jerusalem, which Allen treats as charity rather than church finance.

Allen argues these rules were a deliberate engine of maturity rather than a quirk of circumstance. Because Paul never made a church render him an account of its money, the church learned to carry its own burdens, support its own teachers, and maintain its own poor. He even refuses the common excuse of distrusting native honesty: if the local believers administer their own funds, he says, they administer their own funds, and what becomes of the money "is not our business." To take that responsibility from them is to rob them of one of the best educational experiences a young church can have and to weaken the very sense of mutual responsibility that binds it together.

This is the financial spine of the [[Three-Self Church|self-supporting and self-governing church]] and a primary-source root for the vault's [[2026-05 Kingdom Funding Literature Review|reading of Paul's self-support as a guard against dependency and donor control]]. It sets up the sharp collision examined in [[Tension - Allen Refuses the Standing Subsidy That Gospel Patrons Celebrates|the tension between Allen's refusal of subsidy and the patron model]].
