# Tension - Rinehart Celebrates the Patronage Paul Deliberately Refused

The vault holds two accounts of patronage that use the same word for opposite verdicts, and the collision is real rather than semantic. Rinehart builds [[rinehartGospelPatrons2014|Gospel Patrons]] on the conviction that the benefactor who funds and shelters a frontline worker is a hero the church forgot. He reads Barnabas, the women of Luke 8, and Phoebe as early "Gospel Patrons," and treats the patron-worker bond as a model to recover, defining its essence as "joyful, generous and sacrificial giving; a priority on Word ministry; and a deep relational commitment."

The [[2026-05 Kingdom Funding Literature Review]] reads the same dynamic as a danger Paul engineered against. It argues that Paul's self-support "undercut the patronage dynamics of Greco-Roman culture, where accepting money from a benefactor meant accepting their control," and that by refusing Corinthian money he "preserved his apostolic authority and the church's autonomy simultaneously." On this reading the Philippian relationship Rinehart would file under patronage was "genuine partnership, not patronage," and the review's first conviction is that external funding "tends toward dependency by default, not by accident." Where Rinehart sees the patron as the missing hero, the funding literature sees the patron as the latent controller.

The disagreement is not whether generous giving is good. Both prize it. The fault line runs through the funder's relationship to the worker's independence. Rinehart's celebrated patrons sustained ongoing operations, Lady Huntingdon underwriting 116 preaching places and a seminary, John Thornton buying eleven pulpits to appoint his preferred men, which is precisely the standing external subsidy the review warns produces dependency and donor-shaped incentives. Yet Rinehart blunts the edge himself: he notes Lady Huntingdon usually "encouraged the local people to fund their own buildings" and then added a contribution to launch them, a seed-not-subsidy posture the development critique would actually endorse ([[Donated Goods Function Like a Drug That Recipients Must Quit]]).

The resolution probably lies in distinguishing the soul-aim of the gift from its structural effect, the same split [[Servant Selling Meets the Dependency Critique of Funded Mission]] draws between the ethics of the ask and the ethics of what the money does. Rinehart answers whether the patron's heart and target are right. The funding literature answers whether the funding arrangement leaves the recipient more or less free. A patron can pass Rinehart's test and still fail the review's, which is why the two accounts need each other rather than canceling out. This tension also sits beside [[Tension - Cooper's No-Western-Resources Rule Versus the Vault's Funding Rebuttal]], which contests the same ground from the indigeneity side.
